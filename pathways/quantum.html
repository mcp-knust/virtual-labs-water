Quantum Science & Technology

Qubit driving and decoherence, supported by a ten-station interactive laboratory that carries a class from vector algebra to a variational quantum eigensolver.

The laboratory

Ten stations, one continuous argument

The laboratory is designed so that nothing is asserted without being operable. It opens with the linear algebra every later station depends on, builds electron configurations and chemical bonds, then moves through classical bits to qubits, gates, superposition and entanglement. The final two stations apply the same machinery to a metal–organic framework and to an energy minimisation.

Station map

What each station does

  • Station 01 Vectors + Matrix

    Select 1D, 2D or 3D, then enter your own vector and matrix and watch the transformation.

  • Station 02 Electron Config

    Search all 118 elements by symbol or name and read the resulting orbital occupation.

  • Station 03 Chemical Bonding

    Rotate 3D molecules; see σ and π orbitals, paired spins and ionic electron transfer.

  • Station 04 Bits

    Create a signal from ON and OFF lamps and read the binary number they form.

  • Station 05 Qubit

    Prepare any pure single-qubit state and view it on the Bloch sphere.

  • Station 06 Quantum Gates

    Apply gates using actual complex matrix multiplication.

  • Station 07 Superposition

    Experiment with phase and measurement statistics.

  • Station 08 Entanglement

    Choose two measurement directions and compare the outcomes.

  • Station 09 Pollutant Capture · MOF

    Compare functional groups and pollutant speciation to explore plausible adsorption chemistry.

  • Station 10 VQE Energy

    Run a numerical search for the lowest energy of a small teaching Hamiltonian.

Session deck

Can a net catch everything?

A ten-slide companion deck prepared for World Rivers Day 2026. It makes the case that a physical net only catches what is larger than its mesh, that a smaller particle passes through, and that dissolved contaminants are missed entirely by sand, gravel and activated charcoal — which is the argument for materials designed at the atomic scale.
