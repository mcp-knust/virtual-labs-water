Session 09 · Organic pollution

Chemical Oxygen Demand

COD measures everything oxidisable by hot acid dichromate — biodegradable or not. Paired with BOD5, it gives the biodegradability ratio that decides whether a waste can go to a biological plant or needs chemical pre-treatment.

45 min Dichromate reflux Closed-tube or open reflux Limit: 250 mg/L discharge

Objectives

  • Determine COD by closed-reflux dichromate digestion and ferrous ammonium sulphate titration.
  • Compute COD from blank and sample titres, with attention to sample volume and normality.
  • Use the BOD5/COD ratio to judge biodegradability and select a treatment route.
  • Explain chloride interference and the role of mercury(II) sulphate.

Theory

Organic matter is oxidised by potassium dichromate in strong sulphuric acid at 150 °C for two hours, with silver sulphate as catalyst:

CnH2nOn + Cr2O72− + H+ → CO2 + Cr3+ + H2O

Dichromate is orange; chromium(III) is green, so the digestate darkens with increasing organic load. The excess dichromate is titrated with ferrous ammonium sulphate (FAS) using ferroin indicator:

Cr2O72− + 6Fe2+ + 14H+ → 2Cr3+ + 6Fe3+ + 7H2O
COD (mg/L) = (Vblank − Vsample) × NFAS × 8000 / Vsample
Blank — reagent water carried through the whole digestion; its titre represents the dichromate initially present.
Chloride interference — Cl− is oxidised to Cl2, adding apparent COD; masked with HgSO4 at 10:1 Hg:Cl by mass.
BOD5/COD — above 0.5 readily biodegradable; 0.3–0.5 biodegradable with acclimatisation; below 0.3 poorly biodegradable.

Apparatus and reagents

Apparatus

  • COD digestion tubes or reflux flasks with condensers
  • Block heater or mantle at 150 °C
  • Burette 25 mL, pipettes, safety shield
  • Spectrophotometer if using the colorimetric variant

Reagents

  • Potassium dichromate 0.0417 M (0.25 N)
  • Sulphuric acid with silver sulphate catalyst
  • Mercury(II) sulphate
  • Ferroin indicator, ferrous ammonium sulphate 0.25 N (standardised daily)

Procedure

  1. Homogenise the sample; dilute if COD is expected above 700 mg/L.
  2. Pipette 20 mL sample into a digestion tube; add HgSO4, then dichromate and acid reagent in the specified order, with mixing and cooling between additions.
  3. Prepare a blank with 20 mL reagent water and treat identically.
  4. Reflux at 150 °C for 2 h, then cool to room temperature.
  5. Titrate the blank with FAS to the first reddish-brown; record. Titrate each sample similarly.
  6. Compute COD and, with the paired BOD5, the biodegradability ratio.

Interpretation

COD is always greater than or equal to BOD because it oxidises material that bacteria cannot attack in five days, including lignins, humic substances and many solvents. A large gap between the two is normal for industrial effluent and is the reason a biological plant alone may not meet the discharge limit. For domestic sewage the ratio is typically 0.4–0.6, and a sudden drop in the ratio at an operating plant usually signals an industrial discharge entering the sewer.

Chloride is the classic interference. Sea water or a saline industrial waste can contain 10 000 mg/L chloride, which would add hundreds of mg/L of apparent COD. Mercury(II) sulphate precipitates it as HgCl2, but the reagent is toxic and the dose must be calculated from a measured chloride concentration.

Review questions

1. A 20 mL sample requires 22.40 mL of 0.25 N FAS; the blank requires 25.00 mL. Compute COD.

COD = (25.00 − 22.40) × 0.25 × 8000/20 = 2.60 × 0.25 × 400 = 260 mg/L. That slightly exceeds the 250 mg/L discharge limit, so the effluent would need further treatment or dilution before discharge.

2. Why is COD always greater than BOD for the same sample?

Dichromate at 150 °C oxidises almost all organic carbon, including compounds that are recalcitrant to enzymatic attack within five days, and it also oxidises some inorganic species (chloride, sulphide, nitrite). BOD measures only what the microbial consortium can metabolise in five days at 20 °C, which is a subset. The difference is the non-biodegradable fraction, plus any interference in the COD test.

3. A waste has COD 800 mg/L and BOD₅ 160 mg/L. What does the ratio tell you and what would you propose?

Ratio = 0.2, below 0.3, so the waste is poorly biodegradable and a conventional activated sludge plant would achieve limited removal. Options: chemical oxidation (Fenton or ozone) as a pre-treatment to break recalcitrant molecules, adsorption onto activated carbon for the residual, or anaerobic digestion if the waste is concentrated and warm enough. A treatability test with respirometry would confirm before design.

4. Why must the FAS solution be standardised daily?

Iron(II) is oxidised slowly by dissolved oxygen, so the normality falls over time, especially in a partly filled bottle. Since COD is directly proportional to the FAS normality used in the calculation, an unstandardised solution biases every result. Standardisation against potassium dichromate takes a few minutes and is done on the same day as the samples.
