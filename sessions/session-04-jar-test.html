Jar Test: Coagulation & Flocculation

The jar test is the plant operator's dosing experiment. Six identical samples receive a range of coagulant doses under identical mixing, and the residual turbidity after settling identifies the dose that will work in the full-scale basin.

60 min Alum dose optimisation pH dependence

Objectives

  • Determine the optimum alum dose for a given raw water by residual turbidity.
  • Explain the difference between coagulation (destabilisation) and flocculation (agglomeration).
  • Show how pH shifts the optimum dose and why the operating pH window is narrow.
  • Estimate the sludge produced at the selected dose.

Theory

Colloidal clay particles carry a negative surface charge and repel each other, so they will not settle in a reasonable time. A coagulant such as aluminium sulphate dissociates and hydrolyses:

Al2(SO4)3 · 14H2O + 6HCO3− →
2Al(OH)3(s) + 3SO42− + 6CO2 + 14H2O

The positively charged hydrolysis products adsorb on the negative colloid and reduce the surface potential (charge neutralisation); the Al(OH)3 precipitate also enmeshes particles in a sweep floc. Both mechanisms need mixing: rapid mix disperses the coagulant in seconds, slow mix ("flocculation") lets collisions build floc large enough to settle.

Residual turbidity is a U-shaped function of dose. Below the optimum the colloid remains destabilised only partially; far above it, adsorbed polymer reverses the charge and the suspension is restabilised. The optimum also depends on pH, because the aluminium hydrolysis species distribution changes: the practical window for alum is about pH 5.5–7.5, with the best floc near pH 6.5.

Alkalinity consumed (mg/L as CaCO3) ≈ 0.45 × alum dose (mg/L)

Apparatus and reagents

Apparatus

  • Six-place jar test apparatus with variable-speed paddles
  • Beakers 1000 mL, graduated pipettes, turbidimeter
  • pH meter, stopwatch, thermometer

Reagents

  • Alum stock solution, 10 g/L as Al2(SO4)3·14H2O
  • Lime or sodium hydroxide for pH adjustment
  • 0.1 M hydrochloric acid

Procedure

  1. Fill six 1000 mL beakers with the same raw water and record the raw turbidity, pH and temperature. Adjust pH if the test is also a pH optimisation.
  2. Add the dose series to the beakers while paddles run at 200 rpm for 2 min (rapid mix).
  3. Reduce to 30–40 rpm for 20 min (flocculation). Note floc size and the time it first appears.
  4. Stop the paddles and settle for 30 min without disturbance.
  5. Sample the supernatant about 5 cm below the surface and measure turbidity.
  6. Plot residual turbidity against dose; the minimum is the optimum. Repeat at 2–3 pH values if the operating pH is in question.

Interpretation

Read the optimum from the table of residuals, not from the shape of the floc alone: large floc can still leave fine carry-over. A flat curve means the water is forgiving and the dose can be trimmed for cost; a sharp minimum means close control is needed, and a plant would install turbidity-based dose control rather than rely on a fixed dose.

Diagnostic. If every jar is poor, the problem is usually pH or alkalinity, not dose. If the best jar is still above 5 NTU, add a coagulant aid (polyelectrolyte) or check that the rapid mix is actually reaching 200 rpm at the paddle, not merely at the motor.

Review questions

1. Why does residual turbidity rise again at very high coagulant doses?

Excess hydrolysed aluminium adsorbs on the particles and reverses their charge from negative to positive. The particles then repel each other again (restabilisation) and the suspension cannot flocculate, so residual turbidity climbs. Over-dosing also wastes alkalinity, lowers pH and raises residual aluminium in the finished water.

2. A jar test at pH 7.0 gives a minimum of 8 NTU at 20 mg/L. What would you try next, and why?

Repeat the series at pH 6.0–6.8. The alum hydrolysis species that adsorb most strongly on clay dominate near pH 6–6.5, so shifting the pH often lowers the achievable residual without increasing dose. If pH adjustment is impractical, a polyaluminium chloride or a cationic polymer is the alternative, since both work over a wider pH range.

3. Distinguish rapid mix from flocculation in terms of velocity gradient and purpose.

Rapid mix uses a high velocity gradient (G of 300–1000 s−1) for 10–60 s to disperse the coagulant before hydrolysis products can form in bulk water. Flocculation uses a low gradient (G of 10–70 s−1) for 15–30 min so collisions are gentle enough to build floc without breaking it. Reversing the order produces pin floc that will not settle.

4. Estimate the dry sludge solids from a 25 mg/L alum dose treating 10 000 m3/day.

Alum produces roughly 0.44 kg of Al(OH)3 sludge solids per kg of commercial alum, plus the removed turbidity. 25 mg/L × 10 000 m3/d = 250 kg/d of alum, giving about 110 kg/d of hydroxide solids, to which the captured raw-water solids are added. That is the basis of the sludge-handling design and of the dewatering trial in a later stage of the plant.
