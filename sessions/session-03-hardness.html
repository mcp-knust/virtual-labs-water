Session 03 · Chemical examination

Total Hardness by EDTA Titration

Hardness is the sum of calcium and magnesium, expressed as CaCO3. It determines soap consumption, scale formation in boilers and distribution mains, and the lime dose in softening. This session determines it complexometrically with EDTA.

45 min Complexometric titration Limit: 500 mg/L as CaCO3

Objectives

  • Determine total hardness by titration with disodium EDTA at pH 10.
  • Determine calcium hardness separately at pH 12.5 and obtain magnesium by difference.
  • Classify the water as soft, moderately hard, hard or very hard.
  • Relate hardness to alkalinity and predict scale or corrosion tendency.

Theory

EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, as its disodium salt) forms a 1:1 chelate with Ca2+ and Mg2+. The end point is shown by a metal-ion indicator that is wine-red while bound to calcium or magnesium and free blue when the metal has been taken up by EDTA:

Ca2+ + EDTA4− → [Ca–EDTA]2−   (1:1, pH 10)
Mg2+ + EDTA4− → [Mg–EDTA]2−   (1:1, pH 10)

Because the reaction is 1:1 and calcium carbonate has an equivalent weight of 50 g/eq, one millilitre of 0.010 M EDTA corresponds to 1.00 mg of CaCO3 in a 50 mL sample, that is 20 mg/L:

Hardness (mg/L as CaCO3) = (VEDTA × MEDTA × 100 000) / Vsample
Total hardness — Ca + Mg, titrated at pH 10 with Eriochrome Black T.
Calcium hardness — Ca only, titrated at pH 12.5 with murexide or calcon; Mg precipitates as Mg(OH)2.
Magnesium hardness — total minus calcium.
Carbonate hardness — the portion equal to alkalinity, removed by boiling; the remainder is non-carbonate (permanent) hardness.

Apparatus and reagents

Apparatus

  • Burette 50 mL, conical flasks 250 mL
  • Pipettes 25 and 50 mL, volumetric flask 100 mL
  • Magnetic stirrer and pH meter

Reagents

  • Disodium EDTA, 0.010 M, standardised against CaCO3
  • Ammonia–ammonium chloride buffer, pH 10
  • Eriochrome Black T indicator
  • Sodium hydroxide 1 M (for the calcium titration) and murexide indicator

Procedure

  1. Pipette 50 mL of sample into a conical flask and add 2 mL of pH 10 buffer.
  2. Add a pinch of Eriochrome Black T; the solution turns wine-red.
  3. Titrate with 0.010 M EDTA, swirling continuously, to the first clear blue with no trace of purple. Record Vtotal.
  4. For calcium: take a second 50 mL aliquot, add 2 mL of 1 M NaOH to reach pH 12.5, add murexide, and titrate from orange-red to violet. Record VCa.
  5. Compute total and calcium hardness; magnesium is the difference.
  6. Run a blank on deionised water and subtract if the blank exceeds 0.1 mL.

Why the pH matters. Below pH 10 the EDTA–metal complex is weaker and the end point runs early; above pH 10 magnesium begins to precipitate as hydroxide and is not titrated at all. The buffer holds the pH in the narrow window where both metals are fully complexed.

Interpretation

Hardness below about 60 mg/L as CaCO3 makes water aggressive to cement-lined and metal pipework, because it has little capacity to deposit a protective carbonate film. Above roughly 300 mg/L, calcium carbonate scales heat exchangers and kettles, and soap consumption rises. Where hardness exceeds alkalinity, the excess is non-carbonate hardness (sulphates and chlorides of Ca and Mg) which cannot be removed by boiling and needs lime–soda softening or ion exchange.

Indicator chemistry. Eriochrome Black T is itself a weak acid and its colour depends on pH. If the buffer is omitted the indicator shows a false blue before the true end point, giving a hardness several tens of mg/L too low.

Review questions

1. A 50 mL sample requires 7.35 mL of 0.010 M EDTA. Compute total hardness and classify it.

Hardness = (7.35 × 0.010 × 100 000)/50 = 147 mg/L as CaCO3, which falls in the moderately hard class (75–150). It is a normal supply, compliant with GS 175-1, though it will produce some scale in a kettle.

2. Why is magnesium not titrated at pH 12.5?

At pH 12.5 the solubility product of Mg(OH)2 is exceeded and magnesium precipitates, so it is no longer available to bind the indicator or EDTA. Only calcium is titrated, and magnesium is obtained as total minus calcium. If the precipitate is heavy it can adsorb indicator and bias the calcium result slightly low, so the NaOH is added slowly with swirling.

3. A water has hardness 220 mg/L and alkalinity 140 mg/L as CaCO3. What are the carbonate and non-carbonate fractions?

Carbonate hardness is the smaller of the two, 140 mg/L; non-carbonate hardness is 220 − 140 = 80 mg/L. Boiling removes the carbonate fraction only, so 80 mg/L of permanent hardness remains after boiling — that is the fraction requiring softening.

4. What happens to a hardness result if the EDTA solution has absorbed moisture and is weaker than its nominal 0.010 M?

The titre volume rises, so hardness is reported high if the nominal molarity is used in the calculation. This is why EDTA must be standardised against a calcium carbonate or calcium chloride standard, and why a blank is run. The error is proportional: a solution 2% weaker gives a result 2% high.
