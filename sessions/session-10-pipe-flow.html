Session 10 · Water distribution

Head Loss in Water Distribution Pipes

Distribution design is a trade between capital cost and pumping cost. Pipe diameter sets velocity, velocity sets head loss, and head loss sets the energy bill for the life of the network. This session computes friction and minor losses for a single main and checks the velocity limits.

50 min Darcy–Weisbach Colebrook–White Velocity 0.6–1.5 m/s

Objectives

  • Compute velocity, Reynolds number and friction factor for a flowing main.
  • Apply the Darcy–Weisbach equation with the Colebrook–White friction factor.
  • Add minor losses and compute the hydraulic gradient and pumping power.
  • Check the design against the velocity limits for scour and erosion.

Theory

Head loss in a full pipe has two parts: friction along the barrel and losses at fittings, valves and bends. Darcy–Weisbach is the fundamental form, because the friction factor responds to both roughness and Reynolds number:

hf = f (L/D) (V²/2g)     hm = ΣK (V²/2g)

The friction factor comes from the Colebrook–White equation, which has no closed-form solution and is solved iteratively here:

1/√f = −2 log10[ ε/(3.7D) + 2.51/(Re √f) ]
Re = V D / ν     V = Q / (πD²/4)

For laminar flow (Re < 2300) the friction factor is simply 64/Re and roughness is irrelevant. At a fixed flow, head loss scales approximately as D−5: halving the diameter multiplies the friction loss by about 32. That is why main sizing is dominated by one or two pipe sizes and why upgrading a bottleneck main is so effective.

ε — absolute roughness: uPVC 0.0015 mm, ductile iron 0.06–0.25 mm, concrete 0.3–3 mm, old steel 1–3 mm.
ν — kinematic viscosity, 1.004 × 10−6 m²/s at 20 °C.
Hydraulic gradient — head loss per unit length, usually quoted in m per km.
Velocity limits — below 0.6 m/s solids settle and disinfectant decays unevenly; above 1.5–2.0 m/s erosion, surge and noise become significant.

Apparatus

Apparatus

  • Pipe test rig with interchangeable 25–50 mm pipes
  • Two pressure tappings with manometer or differential transducer
  • Rotameter or electromagnetic flow meter
  • Pump with bypass, thermometer

Procedure outline

  1. Record pipe internal diameter, length and material roughness.
  2. Set a flow and let it stabilise.
  3. Read the differential pressure and convert to head of water.
  4. Repeat over 6–8 flows spanning laminar and turbulent regimes.
  5. Plot hf against V² and derive f; compare with Colebrook–White.
  6. Add a bend or valve and measure the extra loss to obtain K.

Procedure

  1. Measure the internal diameter (not the nominal size) and the distance between tappings.
  2. Open the valve slowly to the first flow setting; release air from the manometer.
  3. Record flow, differential head and water temperature once readings are steady.
  4. Repeat for increasing flows, then decreasing, to check for hysteresis.
  5. Compute V, Re and f for each point; plot the Moody coordinates and compare with the correlation.
  6. Compute the gradient in m/km and compare with the design limit for the network.

Interpretation

For a 150 mm main at 20 L/s the velocity is 1.13 m/s, which is inside the 0.6–1.5 m/s band, and the friction gradient is about 7 m per kilometre — a normal distribution main. Push the same flow through a 100 mm main and the velocity rises to 2.5 m/s, the gradient to well over 30 m/km, and the pumping cost roughly quadruples. Reducing the flow or increasing the diameter is almost always cheaper than adding pump head over the life of the asset.

Two failure modes, opposite directions. Low velocity lets sediment accumulate and can produce discoloured water on the next surge; high velocity erodes cement lining, causes cavitation at fittings and produces water hammer when a valve closes. Both are checked at the design stage, not after commissioning.

Review questions

1. A 200 m long, 150 mm main carries 25 L/s. Taking ε = 0.06 mm and ν = 1.004 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s, estimate the friction loss.

A = π(0.15)²/4 = 0.01767 m², so V = 0.025/0.01767 = 1.41 m/s. Re = 1.41 × 0.15/1.004 × 10−6 = 2.1 × 105 (turbulent). Relative roughness ε/D = 0.06/150 = 4 × 10−4, giving f ≈ 0.0165 from Colebrook–White. Then hf = 0.0165 × (200/0.15) × (1.41²/19.62) ≈ 2.2 m, a gradient of about 11 m/km. That is high for a distribution main and suggests the diameter is marginal at this flow.

2. Why does halving the diameter increase head loss by roughly 32 times at the same flow?

Velocity varies as 1/D², so V² varies as 1/D4. The friction term also contains 1/D, giving hf ∝ 1/D5. Halving D multiplies the loss by 25 = 32. The friction factor changes only slightly, so the scaling is close to exact in the fully rough regime. This is the single most important relationship in main sizing.

3. Why is 0.6 m/s a lower limit in distribution mains?

Below about 0.6 m/s, fine sediment and iron precipitates settle out and form deposits; when the flow later increases, these are resuspended as discoloured water. Low velocity also means long residence times, which allow disinfectant residual to decay and can promote biofilm growth. The exact threshold depends on the solids present, but the operating rule is applied widely.

4. A pump lifts 30 L/s through 800 m of 200 mm main against a 25 m static head. Estimate the shaft power at 70% efficiency.

V = 0.030/0.0314 = 0.95 m/s, Re ≈ 1.9 × 105, f ≈ 0.016 for ductile iron, so hf = 0.016 × (800/0.20) × (0.95²/19.62) ≈ 2.9 m. Add the static head: H ≈ 28 m. Hydraulic power = ρgQH = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.030 × 28 ≈ 8.2 kW; shaft power = 8.2/0.70 ≈ 11.8 kW. Note how the static head, not friction, dominates at this length and diameter.
