Sedimentation: Type I Settling

A settling column converts particle size and density into a settling velocity, and the overflow rate of a basin converts that velocity into a removal efficiency. This is the design link between the laboratory and the clarifier.

60 min Stokes' law Overflow rate Settling column

Objectives

  • Compute the terminal settling velocity of a discrete particle from its diameter and density.
  • Check whether Stokes' law is valid, and correct the velocity when it is not.
  • Relate overflow rate (surface loading) to the removal of a given particle size.
  • Explain why basin area, not depth, controls discrete-particle removal.

Theory

For a smooth sphere falling at terminal velocity in a still fluid, the drag balance gives Stokes' law when the particle Reynolds number is below about 0.5:

vs = g (ρs − ρw) d² / (18 µ)
Re = ρw vs d / µ

Above Re ≈ 0.5 the drag coefficient departs from 24/Re and the velocity must be solved iteratively. The simulation uses the Schiller–Naumann correlation, which stays accurate into the transitional regime:

CD = (24/Re)(1 + 0.15 Re0.687)
v = √[ 4 g d (ρs − ρw) / (3 CD ρw) ]

For an ideal horizontal-flow basin of plan area A treating flow Q, the overflow rate is v0 = Q/A. A particle that settles at vs is completely removed if vs ≥ v0; otherwise the fraction removed is vs/v0, provided it enters at the worst point (the surface at the inlet). Depth cancels out, which is the counter-intuitive result: doubling the depth halves the velocity but doubles the travel time, leaving the removal unchanged.

Removal fraction = min(1, vs / v0)     v0 = Q / A
Type I — discrete, non-flocculating particles; velocity constant during settling.
Type II — flocculent settling; velocity increases as particles agglomerate.
Type III / IV — hindered and compression settling in a sludge blanket.

Apparatus

Apparatus

  • Settling column, 2.0 m, with ports at 0.25 m intervals
  • Stopwatch and 100 mL sampling bottles
  • Turbidimeter or drying oven and balance for gravimetric solids
  • Thermometer; glass spheres or graded sand for calibration

Procedure outline

  1. Disperse the sample uniformly in the column.
  2. At intervals, withdraw 100 mL from a port and measure turbidity or solids.
  3. Plot percentage remaining against time for each depth.
  4. Determine the velocity from the slope of the isoconcentration lines.
  5. Convert to a design overflow rate with the intended basin area.

Procedure

  1. Fill the column with the dispersed sample and start the clock at the moment stirring stops.
  2. Sample at 0.5 m and 1.5 m depth at 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30 and 60 min. Withdraw slowly to avoid disturbing the column.
  3. Measure turbidity of each aliquot and compute the fraction remaining relative to the initial value.
  4. For each depth, find the time at which the fraction remaining first drops — that is the time for the fastest particles to traverse that depth.
  5. Compute velocities as depth/time and plot against particle size or against percentage removed.
  6. Choose a design overflow rate from the removal curve and compute the required plan area for the plant flow.

Interpretation

Sand of 0.2 mm at a specific gravity of 2.65 settles at about 24 mm/s in water at 20 °C, which corresponds to an overflow rate of 86 m/h — far higher than any clarifier. That is why grit removal is a small, short-detention unit while a clarifier for flocculated material, whose effective density is close to water, runs at 0.5–1.0 m/h. The overflow rate is a property of the particle, not of the basin depth.

Reality check. Real basins do not achieve the ideal-curve efficiency. Short-circuiting, wind and density currents, and inlet turbulence reduce performance, which is why design overflow rates are set at roughly 60–70% of the value the settling test suggests.

Review questions

1. Compute the settling velocity of a 0.05 mm particle of specific gravity 2.65 in water at 20 °C, and check Stokes' law.

d = 5 × 10−5 m, ρw = 998 kg/m3, µ = 1.002 × 10−3 Pa·s. Then v = 9.81 × 1652 × (2.5 × 10−9)/(18 × 1.002 × 10−3) ≈ 2.2 × 10−3 m/s (2.2 mm/s). Re = 998 × 0.0022 × 5 × 10−5 / 1.002 × 10−3 ≈ 0.11, which is below 0.5, so Stokes' law is valid and no drag correction is needed.

2. Why does basin depth not affect the removal of a discrete particle?

A deeper basin gives the particle more distance to fall but also a longer detention time, in exactly the same proportion: doubling depth doubles both. The controlling group is Q/A, the overflow rate. In practice depth still matters for flocculation, sludge storage and to reduce the effect of short-circuiting, which is why regulations specify a minimum depth.

3. A basin of 30 m × 12 m treats 8 000 m3/day. What is the overflow rate, and is a 0.02 mm particle removed?

v0 = 8000/(30 × 12) = 22.2 m/d = 0.93 m/h. A 0.02 mm particle of SG 2.65 settles at roughly 0.35 mm/s = 1.26 m/h, which exceeds the overflow rate, so it is completely removed. A 0.01 mm particle at about 0.09 mm/s (0.31 m/h) would be only about one third removed. Note that the second particle is not zero-removal: the fraction v/v0 is removed because it enters part-way down the basin.

4. Why do real clarifiers perform below the ideal removal curve?

Ideal analysis assumes uniform flow, quiescent conditions and particles entering at the surface. Real basins have density currents from temperature differences, wind-driven circulation, inlet jets that carry particles directly to the outlet, and sludge blankets that release gas. These produce a distribution of residence times rather than a single one, so a fraction of the flow short-circuits with a much shorter detention time.
