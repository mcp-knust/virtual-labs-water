Session 06 · Physical treatment

Rapid Sand Filtration

A filter is a compromise between two clocks: the time for head loss to reach the terminal value, and the time for turbidity to break through. Whichever comes first ends the run. This session varies media size, bed depth and rate to see how both clocks move.

55 min Carman–Kozeny Filter run Backwash

Objectives

  • Compute clean-bed head loss from media size, bed depth and filtration rate.
  • Predict head-loss development and the terminal head-limited run time.
  • Identify whether a run ends by head loss or by turbidity breakthrough.
  • Size a backwash for 20–30% bed expansion.

Theory

Rapid gravity filters are depth filters: particles are removed throughout the bed by interception, sedimentation onto grain surfaces and, for small particles, diffusion. The clean bed obeys the Carman–Kozeny equation, written here for the head loss through a bed of spheres of diameter d and porosity e:

h0 / L = 180 µ v (1 − e)² / (ρ g d² e³)

Because head loss scales as 1/d², media size dominates: a 20% increase in effective size cuts clean bed head loss by roughly 30%. As deposits accumulate, the bed's effective porosity falls and head loss grows roughly linearly with the mass deposited, which is proportional to influent solids and to the volume filtered:

h(t) = h0 + k t     k ∝ (Cin v / L)

The run ends when either the available head (typically 2.0–2.5 m above the media) is exhausted, or when turbidity appears in the filtrate — breakthrough — because the deposit has penetrated the full depth of the bed. Breakthrough comes sooner with coarse media, shallow beds, high rates and high influent solids.

trun = min( (hterminal − h0) / k , tbreakthrough )
Effective size (d10) — sieve size passing 10% of the media by mass.
Uniformity coefficient — d60/d10; below 1.5 is preferred, since a wide range stratifies and lets fines to the top.
L/d ratio — bed depth divided by media size; should exceed 1000 to prevent breakthrough.
Backwash — upflow at 40–50 m/h for 5–10 min, expanding the bed 20–30% to release the deposit.

Apparatus

Apparatus

  • Filter column, 100 mm diameter, with manometer tappings above and below the media
  • Sand media, d10 0.5–1.2 mm, and graded gravel support
  • Constant-head tank, rotameter, turbidimeter
  • Backwash supply with flow control

Procedure outline

  1. Backwash the clean bed and record the clean-bed head loss at three rates.
  2. Run at constant rate with a known influent turbidity.
  3. Log head loss and effluent turbidity every 30 min.
  4. Stop the run at 2.0 m head or at 1 NTU effluent, whichever is first.
  5. Backwash, measure expansion, and repeat with a different media size.

Procedure

  1. Fluidise the bed with backwash water at increasing rate until the bed is fully expanded; record the rate at which expansion first appears.
  2. Set the filtration rate with the outlet valve and confirm it with the rotameter.
  3. Record the initial (clean-bed) manometer difference at that rate.
  4. Start dosing the synthetic influent at the chosen turbidity and start the clock.
  5. Record head loss, effluent turbidity and flow every 30 min.
  6. End the run at the terminal head or at the turbidity limit, and compute the unit run volume (m3/m²).

Interpretation

A well-designed filter has a run time of 24–48 h and an L/d ratio above 1000. If the run ends by head loss well before 20 h, the media is too fine or the influent solids load is too high, and the fix is a coarser media, a deeper bed, or better upstream clarification. If it ends by breakthrough, the bed is too shallow for the rate, or the media is too coarse; the fix is the opposite. Increasing the rate shortens the run in both directions at once, which is why rate changes are made cautiously.

Backwash warning. Insufficient backwash leaves the bed partially clogged, so the next run starts with an elevated clean-bed head loss and ends early. Excessive backwash washes out the fines, raising d10 and promoting breakthrough. Both faults look like "the filter is failing".

Review questions

1. Compute the clean-bed head loss for d = 0.8 mm, L = 0.9 m, e = 0.42, v = 12 m/h at 20 °C.

v = 12/3600 = 3.33 × 10−3 m/s; d = 8 × 10−4 m; µ = 1.002 × 10−3 Pa·s. h0/L = 180 × 1.002 × 10−3 × 3.33 × 10−3 × 0.3364 / (998 × 9.81 × 6.4 × 10−7 × 0.0741) ≈ 0.54. So h0 = 0.54 × 0.9 ≈ 0.49 m. That is a reasonable clean-bed value; a new filter should sit below about 0.3–0.5 m.

2. Why does a filter run end by breakthrough rather than head loss when the media is coarse?

Coarse media have large pores, so the deposit penetrates deeper and the head loss for the same solids load is lower. The bed therefore has head capacity to spare when particles begin to emerge in the filtrate, because the deposit has reached the bottom of the bed. The run ends on quality, not on hydraulics. The remedy is a deeper bed or a dual-media (anthracite over sand) configuration, which puts coarse, high-capacity media on top of finer media that polish the filtrate.

3. Why is the L/d ratio required to exceed about 1000?

The ratio is a surrogate for the number of grain layers a particle must pass. Below about 1000, there is a risk that a crack or a locally fluidised zone allows a path through the bed with few contacts, and turbidity appears almost immediately — a "breakthrough" that is really a design deficiency rather than a capacity limit.

4. What backwash rate would you set for a 0.8 mm sand bed, and what would you check afterwards?

Start at about 40 m/h and increase until the bed is visibly expanded by 20–30%, typically 45–50 m/h at 20 °C; colder water needs a lower rate because viscosity is higher. Afterwards check that the clean-bed head loss has returned to its baseline, that no media has been washed into the troughs, and that the first filtrate after backwash is run to waste until turbidity falls below 1 NTU (filter ripening).
