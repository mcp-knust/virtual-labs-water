pH, Acidity & Alkalinity

Alkalinity is the capacity of water to neutralise acid, and it is the single most important number for treatment control: it fixes coagulant dose, buffer capacity and the Langelier index. This session determines it by two-endpoint titration and resolves the result into hydroxide, carbonate and bicarbonate fractions.

50 min Potentiometric / indicator titration Limit: pH 6.5–8.5

Objectives

  • Calibrate and use a pH meter; check it against at least two buffers.
  • Determine phenolphthalein alkalinity (P) and total alkalinity (T) by titration with standard acid.
  • Resolve P and T into hydroxide, carbonate and bicarbonate alkalinity.
  • Explain why alkalinity, not pH alone, controls coagulant dosing.

Theory

Alkalinity is an operational quantity: a water is alkaline if it consumes acid down to a chosen equivalence point. The two standard points are pH 8.3, where phenolphthalein turns colourless and essentially all hydroxide has been neutralised and carbonate has been converted to bicarbonate, and pH 4.5, where methyl orange changes and bicarbonate is fully converted to carbonic acid.

CO32− + H+ → HCO3−   (to pH 8.3)
HCO3− + H+ → H2CO3   (to pH 4.5)

Results are expressed as mg/L CaCO3, because the equivalent weight of CaCO3 (50 g/eq) is the conventional divisor:

Alkalinity (mg/L as CaCO3) = (Vacid × Nacid × 50 000) / Vsample

The pair (P, T) determines the species present. Hydroxide and bicarbonate cannot coexist in significant concentration, because they neutralise each other:

The simulation solves the carbonate system directly rather than applying an empirical curve. For a closed system with total inorganic carbon CT, the charge balance is

SID + [H+] = [OH−] + [HCO3−] + 2[CO32−]

where SID is the strong-ion difference. Adding acid lowers SID by one equivalent per mole of H+ added, and the equation is solved for [H+] by bisection. That is why the titrated curve in the simulation bends where a real carbonate titration bends.

Apparatus and reagents

Apparatus

  • pH meter with combination electrode, buffers pH 4.0, 7.0 and 10.0
  • Burette, 50 mL, class A, with 0.1 mL graduations
  • Conical flasks 250 mL, measuring cylinders, magnetic stirrer

Reagents

  • Standard sulfuric acid, 0.02 N (0.01 M)
  • Phenolphthalein indicator, 0.5% in ethanol
  • Methyl orange / bromocresol green mixed indicator
  • Sodium carbonate primary standard for acid standardisation

Procedure

  1. Calibrate the pH meter with pH 7.0 and pH 4.0 buffers; slope must be within 95–105%.
  2. Measure 50 mL of sample into a conical flask. Add 3 drops of phenolphthalein. If the solution is pink, titrate with 0.02 N acid to the disappearance of colour (pH 8.3) and record VP.
  3. Add 3 drops of methyl orange indicator to the same flask and continue titrating to the first orange-red (pH 4.5). Record the total volume VT.
  4. Compute P and T as CaCO3, then resolve the species from the table above.
  5. Report pH separately, measured before any titration, with the meter temperature noted.

Interpretation

A water with high alkalinity resists pH change, which is usually what a treatment plant wants: coagulant addition and chlorination both depress pH, and an unbuffered water can crash below pH 5. But high alkalinity also means high hardness and scale potential. The operational target is generally 40–120 mg/L as CaCO3 for coagulation control; below about 20 mg/L the water is poorly buffered and lime or caustic must be dosed before alum.

Common error. Titrating past the methyl orange endpoint, or reading the burette from the wrong meniscus, biases T high. The endpoint is the first persistent orange-red, not a deep red; a deep red means the sample has been over-titrated by several tenths of a millilitre, which is 5–10 mg/L as CaCO3 at 50 mL sample volume.

Review questions

1. A 50 mL sample requires 1.2 mL of 0.02 N acid to pH 8.3 and 6.4 mL in total to pH 4.5. Compute P, T and the species present.

P = (1.2 × 0.02 × 50 000)/50 = 24 mg/L; T = (6.4 × 0.02 × 50 000)/50 = 128 mg/L. Since P < T/2, there is no hydroxide: carbonate = 2P = 48 mg/L and bicarbonate = T − 2P = 80 mg/L. Check: 48 + 80 = 128 mg/L. This is a typical bicarbonate-dominated groundwater with a small carbonate component.

2. Why can hydroxide and bicarbonate alkalinity not both be present in a reported result?

They neutralise each other quantitatively: OH− + HCO3− → CO32− + H2O, with an equilibrium constant around 1010. Any water containing both would react until one is exhausted, so a result showing both is a calculation error, not a water property. In the P > T/2 case the hydroxide is present and bicarbonate is zero.

3. A plant doses 40 mg/L alum into water with 15 mg/L alkalinity. Predict what happens and state the fix.

Alum consumes alkalinity at roughly 0.45–0.5 mg/L as CaCO3 per mg/L of alum, so 40 mg/L would consume about 18–20 mg/L — more than the 15 mg/L available. pH collapses below the coagulation optimum (about 6.0–6.5 for alum), floc formation fails, and residual aluminium rises. The fix is to add lime or sodium hydroxide to raise alkalinity before or with the coagulant, or to switch to a pre-hydrolysed coagulant such as polyaluminium chloride, which consumes less alkalinity.

4. Why is pH reported as a separate measurement rather than being inferred from alkalinity?

Alkalinity is a capacity, pH is an intensity. Two waters can have identical alkalinity and very different pH depending on the CO2 content: a closed groundwater at 100 mg/L alkalinity may sit at pH 7.3, while the same water after aeration and CO2 stripping sits at pH 8.4. Since pH governs coagulation, corrosion and disinfection efficiency, it must be measured directly.
