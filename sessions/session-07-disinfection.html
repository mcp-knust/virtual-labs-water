Session 07 · Disinfection

Breakpoint Chlorination

Chlorine added to water containing ammonia is consumed before any free residual appears. The dose–residual curve has a peak of combined chlorine and then a sharp dip called the breakpoint. Operating on the wrong side of it wastes chlorine and under-disinfects.

45 min Chlorine demand Ct concept Target: 0.2–1.0 mg/L free

Objectives

  • Determine the chlorine demand, breakpoint dose and free residual for a given ammonia concentration.
  • Explain the reactions that produce combined chlorine and destroy it at the breakpoint.
  • Compute the Ct product and estimate disinfection credit.
  • Select an operating dose for a target free residual at the tap.

Theory

In water at pH 7–8, chlorine exists as hypochlorous acid and hypochlorite in proportions set by the dissociation constant (pKa 7.5):

Cl2 + H2O ↔ HOCl + HCl     HOCl ↔ H+ + OCl−

HOCl is the far stronger disinfectant because it is uncharged and penetrates the cell wall. If ammonia is present, chlorine reacts with it in sequence:

NH3 + HOCl → NH2Cl + H2O   (monochloramine)
2NH2Cl + HOCl → N2 + 3HCl + H2O   (breakpoint)

The stoichiometry is about 7.6 mg of chlorine per mg of ammonia nitrogen at the breakpoint, which is why a water with 0.5 mg/L NH3–N needs roughly 3.8 mg/L before any free chlorine survives. Up to about 5:1 a combined residual builds; beyond that the chloramines are destroyed and the residual falls to almost zero at 7.6:1; past the breakpoint every additional milligram appears as free chlorine.

Dbreakpoint ≈ 7.6 × [NH3–N]     Ct = residual × contact time

Disinfection is not a function of concentration alone. The product of residual and contact time (Ct, in mg·min/L) predicts inactivation, with a temperature correction: cold water needs a much larger Ct for the same log removal. Typical free-chlorine Ct for 3-log Giardia inactivation is roughly 230 mg·min/L at 10 °C and 100 mg·min/L at 20 °C.

Apparatus and reagents

Apparatus

  • Six 500 mL chlorine demand bottles, glass-stoppered
  • DPD colorimeter or amperometric titrator
  • Burette and pipettes for hypochlorite dosing
  • pH meter, thermometer, stopwatch

Reagents

  • Sodium hypochlorite stock, standardised (about 1 g/L as Cl2)
  • DPD free and total chlorine reagents
  • Ammonium chloride standard for spiking
  • Dechlorinating agent (sodium thiosulphate) for safe disposal

Procedure

  1. Measure the ammonia nitrogen concentration of the sample (Nessler or phenate method).
  2. Prepare a dose series spanning 0 to about 12 mg/L as Cl2, in 500 mL bottles.
  3. Mix and hold for the chosen contact time at constant temperature, in the dark.
  4. Measure free and total chlorine by DPD. Combined chlorine is total minus free.
  5. Plot total and free residual against dose; locate the peak and the breakpoint minimum.
  6. Select the operating dose as the breakpoint plus the target free residual, then check the Ct against the required value for the target organism and temperature.

Interpretation

An operating dose just past the breakpoint is unstable: any rise in ammonia moves the breakpoint and the residual collapses. Plants therefore either strip ammonia before chlorination, chloraminate deliberately (holding a combined residual of 1–2 mg/L as a stable secondary disinfectant), or dose to breakpoint plus a margin and monitor free chlorine continuously. The margin has a cost: every milligram above breakpoint is a milligram that forms trihalomethanes with natural organic matter.

Safety. Never mix hypochlorite with acid: chlorine gas is released. Dose into water, never into concentrated acid, and keep the dosing point downstream of any acid or coagulant injection.

Review questions

1. A water contains 0.6 mg/L ammonia nitrogen. Estimate the breakpoint dose and the dose for 0.5 mg/L free residual.

Breakpoint ≈ 7.6 × 0.6 = 4.6 mg/L as Cl2. For 0.5 mg/L free residual the dose must exceed the breakpoint by that residual, so about 5.1 mg/L. In practice a margin is added for ammonia variability, and the actual dose is set from the measured curve rather than the stoichiometric ratio.

2. Why does the residual fall almost to zero at the breakpoint?

At the breakpoint, chlorine oxidises the chloramines themselves: monochloramine is attacked to release nitrogen gas and chloride, so both the combined residual and the applied chlorine are consumed. The residual is minimal precisely because the oxidant is being used up in destroying the ammonia rather than remaining as a measurable species. Adding more chlorine then accumulates as free HOCl/OCl−.

3. Why is Ct used instead of residual alone?

Inactivation is a time-integrated exposure: a low residual for a long time can be as effective as a high residual for a short time. Ct captures that trade-off and is the basis of regulatory disinfection credit. The relationship is not perfectly linear (the Chick–Watson model uses an exponent on concentration), but Ct tables are the practical design tool.

4. Why is free chlorine more effective than the same concentration of monochloramine?

HOCl is small, uncharged and diffuses readily through the cell envelope, and it reacts with a wide range of intracellular targets. Monochloramine is bulkier, less reactive and must be actively taken up, so it inactivates more slowly — often by a factor of 40–100 in Ct terms. Its advantage is persistence: it decays much more slowly in distribution, which is why it is used as a secondary disinfectant.
