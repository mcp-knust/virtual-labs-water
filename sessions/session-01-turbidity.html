Session 01 · Physical examination of water

Turbidity & Apparent Colour

Suspended matter scatters and absorbs light. This session measures that effect nephelometrically, in nephelometric turbidity units (NTU), and measures colour against the platinum–cobalt scale, in true colour units (TCU).

45 min Nephelometry Spectrophotometry Limits: 5 NTU · 15 TCU

Objectives

  • Measure the turbidity of a water sample with a nephelometer and correct for dilution.
  • Distinguish true colour from apparent colour and convert absorbance to Pt–Co units.
  • Judge both results against GS 175-1 and the WHO drinking-water guidelines.
  • Explain why turbidity is an operational, not merely an aesthetic, parameter.

Theory

Turbidity is an optical property: light passing through a sample is scattered by particles whose refractive index differs from water, and absorbed by coloured dissolved matter. Because scattering depends on particle size, shape and refractive index, turbidity is not a gravimetric measure of suspended solids — two samples with the same suspended-solids concentration can read very different NTU.

A nephelometer measures light scattered at 90° to the incident beam. The scale is defined by formazin, a polymer suspension; a 4000 NTU stock is diluted to give the working standards, so the unit is instrument-independent only because the calibration material is fixed.

NTUoriginal = NTUmeasured × DF     DF = Vtotal / Vsample

Dilution is required when a sample reads above the instrument range and for very turbid raw waters where multiple scattering makes the response non-linear. Diluting also risks changing the particle-size distribution, so dilute with turbidity-free water and report the dilution.

Colour is measured against chloroplatinate standards and expressed as Pt–Co units, where 1 TCU equals 1 mg/L platinum as chloroplatinate ion. Absorbance follows the Beer–Lambert law:

A = log10(I0/I) = ε · b · c   →   cTCU = A / (ε · b)
Apparent colour — measured on the unfiltered sample; includes the turbidity contribution.
True colour — measured after filtration through 0.45 µm, which removes turbidity.

Apparatus and reagents

Apparatus

  • Nephelometric turbidimeter, formazin-calibrated
  • Sample cells, optically matched, scratch-free
  • Spectrophotometer, 456 nm, 1–5 cm cells
  • Membrane filtration unit, 0.45 µm
  • Nessleriser with Pt–Co disc or standards

Reagents

  • Formazin stock, 4000 NTU
  • Turbidity-free water (0.22 µm filtered)
  • Chloroplatinate colour standards, 0–100 TCU
  • Potassium hexachloroplatinate(IV) and cobalt(II) chloride for standard preparation

Procedure

  1. Bring samples to room temperature; invert gently to resuspend settled solids. Do not shake vigorously — that entrains air bubbles, which read as turbidity.
  2. Rinse the sample cell twice with the sample, fill to the mark, and wipe the outside with lint-free tissue. Handle by the cap only.
  3. Insert the cell with the orientation mark aligned to the instrument; read NTU after the display stabilises (about 10 s).
  4. If the reading exceeds the range or the calibration curve, dilute a measured volume with turbidity-free water and re-read. Record sample volume, final volume and dilution factor.
  5. For colour: filter a portion through 0.45 µm, discarding the first 25 mL, and read absorbance at 456 nm against turbidity-free water.
  6. Convert absorbance to TCU using the calibration in force for the cell path length used.

Calculation

Worked example. A sample reads 78.0 NTU after diluting 25 mL to 100 mL.

DF = 100 / 25 = 4.0   →   NTU = 78.0 × 4.0 = 312 NTU

The corrected value is the one reported, together with the dilution: 312 NTU (1:4 dilution). Reporting the diluted reading alone understates the load by a factor of four.

Note on the pair of measurements. If apparent colour is measured before filtration, part of the reading is turbidity. The difference between apparent and true colour is a rough index of the suspended fraction — useful when a plant is trying to separate dissolved colour from carry-over floc.

Review questions

1. Why does a nephelometer measure scattered light at 90° rather than the light transmitted straight through?

At 90° the detector sees almost only scattered light, so the signal rises with particle concentration from a near-zero background. A transmission measurement would instead show a small decrease on a large baseline, which is far less sensitive at low turbidity. The 90° geometry is also the one used to define the formazin NTU scale, so readings are comparable between instruments that follow the same design.

2. A sample is diluted 25 mL to 250 mL and reads 19.6 NTU. What is the turbidity of the original?

DF = 250/25 = 10, so NTU = 19.6 × 10 = 196 NTU. Report it as "196 NTU (1:10 dilution)". Note that the original is well above the 5 NTU limit and the water would require full coagulation, sedimentation and filtration rather than simple chlorination.

3. Turbidity is described as an operational parameter, not only an aesthetic one. Explain.

Particles shield micro-organisms from disinfectant, exert chlorine demand, and protect attached biofilms in distribution mains. Turbidity also indicates that filtration or coagulation has failed, and it interferes with disinfection monitoring because it consumes and scatters light used by residual analysers. Hence the operational target is well below the aesthetic limit — ideally under 1 NTU, and below 0.3 NTU for filtered supplies.

4. Why is true colour measured after 0.45 µm filtration, and what error arises if the filter is not rinsed?

Filtration removes the particulate fraction so the reading reflects dissolved chromophores only. The first portion of filtrate carries loose filter material and adsorbed matter, which adds both turbidity and absorbance; discarding the first 25 mL prevents a falsely high result.

5. Your sample reads 6.4 NTU and 22 TCU. Which parameters fail, and what unit process would you recommend first?

Both fail the aesthetic limits (5 NTU, 15 TCU), turbidity only marginally. The paired failure suggests colloidal material that is not settling, so the first step is jar testing for coagulant dose and pH (Session 04), followed by sedimentation and filtration. Disinfection should not be relied on while turbidity is elevated.
